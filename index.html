What it measures

Each time a ball lands it keeps a fraction of its speed. That fraction is called the coefficient of restitution, and here it is called the bounciness score. Because flight time follows speed, every gap between bounce sounds is the previous gap times that same fraction. The app times the gaps to about a millisecond and works out the score. More air pressure means a springier ball and a higher score.

Made for ED5518 Data Visualization for Engineers (IIT Madras), Project 2, Challenge 5: measure a real physical condition with a phone and make a visual that helps someone identify it. See About for the method, labels, limits and course links.

Check the app hears bounces

Three ways to confirm it works on your phone before you trust a reading.

1. One-tap self-tests on this phone

These feed made-up bounce sounds straight into the analysis (no microphone) and show the result with what should happen. Use them to see a good reading and a refusal in seconds.

2. Known-bounce check through the microphone

This checks the real microphone path. It plays a made-up bounce sound whose score you choose. Play it from a laptop or a second phone, about 30 cm from the phone running Check a ball. The reading should match the chosen score within 0.01.

Order: press Start listening on the listening phone first, then Play here. You can also download the file and use Analyse a sound file on the Check tab.

3. Tape check on a real drop

After each real drop the app estimates how high the ball was dropped from, using only the first gap. Measure your drop height with a tape. The two should agree within about 20% (real balls get slightly bouncier as they slow down, which pulls the estimate low). If you set a drop height in your plan, the app checks this itself and refuses drops that don't fit.

4. If it doesn't hear the bounces

  • Level bar doesn't move: the mic is blocked. On iPhone, open Settings, then Safari (or Chrome), then Microphone, and set it to Allow or Ask. On Android, tap the icon left of the address, then Permissions, then Microphone, Allow.
  • Opened from a chat or mail app: use its menu to open the link in Chrome or Safari.
  • Bar moves but few ticks appear: put the phone 50 cm to 1 m away, drop onto tile or stone from about 1 m, and switch off fans.
  • Too loud warning: move the phone further away, to 1 to 2 m.
  • Heard one sound, then nothing: press Start first, walk to your mark, then drop. The app waits up to 20 seconds.
  • Still nothing: record the drop with a voice recorder and use Analyse a sound file on the Check tab. On iPhone, share the Voice Memo to Files first. Some Android recorders save .amr or .3gp, which browsers can't open; choose .m4a or .wav.

About this app

The question it answers: which pump pressure is this ball closest to, judged only by the sound of its bounces?

Summary for AI readers

  • Purpose: classify a ball's inflation pressure from a phone microphone recording of it bouncing.
  • Sensor: microphone through the browser (Web Audio), Android and iPhone, nothing installed.
  • Measured feature: coefficient of restitution e from the gaps between bounce sounds (gap ratio), shown as the bounciness score.
  • Label: pressure gauge reading taken before each drop, filmed.
  • Visual: barcode comparator (unknown drawn in every reference row) plus a score strip with reference ranges.
  • Test without a ball: Test tab, one-tap self-tests and a known-bounce sound.

Who it's for

Coaches and sports-room staff who check balls before a game. Basketball rules define a correctly pumped ball by how high it rebounds, and every ball is printed with its recommended pressure. A gauge is not always at hand; a phone usually is.

How it works

When a ball lands it keeps a fraction e of its speed. Time in the air is proportional to speed, so each gap between bounce sounds is e times the gap before:

gapn+1 = e × gapn

The app filters out hum and rumble, finds the sharp start of each bounce sound, and accepts only sounds with a broadband click, as an impact makes. It then picks the run of sounds whose gaps shrink steadily, ignoring stray knocks, and fits a straight line to log(gap) against bounce number over the first six gaps. The slope gives e, shown as the bounciness score. The score uses timing only; loudness matters just for whether each bounce is heard. A ball with more air is springier and loses less energy per bounce, so its score is higher. Allen and Allen measured a basketball going from 0.41 when flat to 0.87 at 13 psi. Very soft balls (score below about 0.5) give too few bounces to measure.

Drop height changes the first gap but not the ratio between gaps. The barcode is scaled so the first gap is always one unit, and it shows only the bounces used for the score, so drops from slightly different heights and in noisier rooms still line up. The score drifts a little with impact speed, so the drop height stays fixed.

How to read the picture

  • Bounce rhythm: one row per reference pressure. Grey lines are reference drops, one tick per bounce. The drop being judged is orange, repeated in every row, with faint orange guides through the grey. Find the row where they match. A soft ball gives a short, bunched barcode; a well pumped one runs longer.
  • Bounciness score: every drop is a dot on one shared scale. Grey dots are references, grouped by pressure. The orange line is the drop being judged. The shaded regions are the app's own rule, each group's range split halfway between neighbouring groups, so a reader using them is using the picture plus that rule.

How the labels are made

The pressure levels, ball, floor and drop height are fixed in a plan before any recording (the app's Data tab locks it, and PLAN.md is committed to the website's repository). Each drop is labelled by a pressure gauge reading taken just before it, filmed in the same shot. A new setup means letting air out and pumping back to the level; repeated drops on one setup are not counted as independent. Held-out test recordings come from a separate session, never feed the reference ranges, carry opaque IDs, and are listed in shuffled order. Their true pressures go in a separate file, c5_test_truth.csv, which is kept off this website.

Uncertainty and what can go wrong

  • Each score comes with a spread from the fit. The honest uncertainty is the spread between repeated setups at one pressure, which the grey dots show.
  • Floor type changes the score by about as much as a small pressure step, so references only apply to the same floor, ball and drop height.
  • A cold ball has lower pressure and bounces lower. Let it reach room temperature.
  • Spin, a sideways drop or the ball hitting a wall breaks the steady shrink. The app checks for this and refuses.
  • Background noise can hide quiet late bounces, and a person knocking with steadily shrinking gaps can look like a ball. Setting the drop height in the plan lets the app catch the second case.
  • iPhone browsers may still apply some audio processing. Timing survives it, which is why the score uses only timing.
  • Treat the result as a match to the nearest reference group. A gauge is still the true reading.

A drop that fails any check gets No reading with the reason and the fix. On our synthetic attack set of about 450 recordings (stray knocks, ringing balls, hum, reverb, knocks with no ball), 5 knock-only recordings still got a score; every clean drop was read correctly. Refused drops are logged on the Data tab and exported as failure cases.

For graders

  • Credible physical evidence: gauge reading filmed before each drop; plan locked before recording; known-bounce and tape checks of the method.
  • Readers on held-out recordings: Compare tab and reader test; test recordings from a separate session with opaque, shuffled IDs; the truth file is kept off the website.
  • Repeatable, with uncertainty and failures: anyone can build their own reference set with their ball on the Data tab; self-tests on the Test tab; refusal rules; held-out accuracy with a Wilson interval on the Data tab; logged failed drops.
  • Submitted files: measurement CSV, this page as the visual, c5_test_truth.csv, a video of under a minute, and a note on how labels are known.

How this maps to ED5518

  • Decision first (Week 4) and a verdict in under 10 seconds (Week 8): one question, one score, the reference group it falls in. The 10 second target is checked in the reader test.
  • Position on a common scale (Week 2, Cleveland and McGill): every drop is a position on one shared axis, and all barcodes share one time axis. No dials or colour-only verdicts.
  • One highlight colour against grey (Week 2): orange is always the drop being judged.
  • The right comparison group (Week 3, Challenger): each drop is shown against every pressure at once, never against one level alone.
  • Tested envelope versus required envelope (Week 5, midsem Q11): the grey ranges are the tested envelope on this floor. The basketball rule's rebound band was set on a different floor, so it is not drawn on the chart.
  • Refuse beyond the evidence (Week 5, Draupner) and sensors have their own envelopes (Week 6): uneven, noisy or too-short drops get No reading, and scores outside the reference range are flagged.
  • Every summary is a selection (Week 7): the score ignores loudness and temperature on purpose, and the limits above say so.
  • Verification (Week 9): self-tests, known-bounce sounds, a tape check of drop height, held-out recordings from another session, a blind reader test with new people, and the app rule's held-out accuracy with a Wilson interval.
  • Visual comparison research: the unknown is drawn inside every reference row (juxtaposition plus superposition, Gleicher et al. 2011; L'Yi et al. 2020). All pressures are visible together, since contrast between categories helps people learn them (Hatala et al. 2003; Carvalho and Goldstone 2014). Labelled regions on a number line follow Zikmund-Fisher et al. 2017.
